The photograph that thinks it is a document

A scanned PDF shows you a document but behaves like a photograph of one. Search finds nothing, because there are no characters to find — only pixels arranged like characters. Copy-paste lifts an image, screen readers fall silent, and the archive that holds ten thousand such files holds ten thousand opaque pictures whose contents no system can index. OCR — optical character recognition — is the technology that closes that gap: it examines the page image, recognises letterforms, and reconstructs the text the page depicts, restoring the document to the world of searchable, copyable, computable words.

PDFSeer runs that recognition on your own device and returns two artefacts: the recognised text itself, as a clean .txt file, and a searchable PDF in which an invisible text layer is laid in register beneath each scanned page image. The searchable file looks identical to your scan — but Ctrl+F now works, selections copy words, and indexers can finally read your archive. It is the single most valuable transformation a scanned document can undergo.

How the searchable layer is built

Each page is rendered at double scale for the recogniser's benefit — OCR accuracy climbs steeply with resolution up to a point — and passed to the Tesseract engine, the open-source recogniser whose lineage runs from Hewlett-Packard research to Google stewardship, now running entirely in your browser. Tesseract segments the page into lines and words, classifies the shapes, and returns not only text but coordinates: where on the page each line lives.

Those coordinates are what make a true searchable PDF rather than a text dump wearing a PDF coat. PDFSeer writes each recognised line as real text positioned behind the page image, at the line's own location and approximate size, drawn in white beneath the picture so it never shows. Viewers and search engines read that hidden layer as the page's text — because mechanically, it is. The visible page remains the scan, pixel for pixel, which is why the method is trusted for records: nothing about the evidence changes, and everything about its findability does.

Accuracy: what OCR promises and what it cannot

On clean input — a straight, high-contrast scan of printed text — modern OCR is startlingly good, routinely above ninety-eight percent character accuracy. Accuracy decays with the real world's imperfections: skewed pages, faded or foxed paper, show-through from the reverse side, cramped type, decorative fonts, handwriting (a different discipline, where results are honest but modest), and tables whose geometry confuses line-finding. The tool's language selector matters enormously: telling the engine the document is Hindi or German loads the right letterforms and dictionary, and a wrong language choice is the commonest self-inflicted accuracy wound.

Treat OCR output as an excellent draft, not a transcript of record. For searching, indexing and reading, small errors are irrelevant — search tolerates a misread letter in a long word poorly, but across a whole archive the recognised majority transforms retrieval. For republication, legal evidence or figures that matter, proofread against the scan. PDFSeer states this plainly in its own completion message, because a tool that oversells OCR accuracy teaches its users to skip the one check that matters.

How to OCR a PDF, step by step

Load the scanned file and choose the document's language correctly — this single setting decides more accuracy than everything else combined. Run the OCR and watch pages process in sequence; the recognised text accumulates in the panel so you can judge quality from page one rather than discovering it at the end. Long documents take a few seconds per page on your device, and the progress bar counts honestly.

When it completes, download both outputs. Search the .txt for a distinctive word you know is on page three — if it is found, the pipeline worked. Open the searchable PDF, press Ctrl+F, and search again inside the document itself: the viewer should jump to the word on its scanned page, highlighted over the image. That small jump is one of the more satisfying moments in document work — the archive, waking up.

Where searchable PDFs change the game

Offices digitising filing cabinets gain archives they can actually interrogate: 'every invoice mentioning this supplier, across six years of scans'. Researchers OCR proceedings and out-of-print texts into corpora that can be mined and quoted. Legal and compliance teams make disclosure bundles searchable for reviewers instead of ceremonial. Accessibility is transformed: a screen-reader user gains a document that was previously a locked picture, which for public records is not a convenience but a right being operationalised.

The compounding effect is the real story. One searchable PDF is a nicer file; a thousand searchable PDFs are a database wearing a filing cabinet's clothes. Institutions sit on mountains of scanned paper whose knowledge is effectively frozen, and the thaw costs nothing but compute they already own — which, since this tool runs locally, means no per-page OCR invoices standing between an archive and its own contents.

Preparing scans OCR will love

Five minutes at the scanner saves an hour of proofreading. Scan at 300 dpi in grayscale, straighten the page against the scanner's edge, and keep the glass clean — specks become punctuation. Avoid photographing pages when a scanner exists: perspective and shadows are accuracy poison. For bound books, press the spine flat or accept that words near the gutter will suffer, and consider splitting double-page spreads so each page is recognised on its own geometry. If a page returns garbled, re-scan just that page better rather than accepting the errors — OCR quality is decided at capture, and no downstream setting fully redeems a bad image. Your archive deserves the good scan; it will be read by machines for decades.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 'searchable PDF' actually mean?

The file looks like your scan, but a real text layer sits hidden beneath each page image, aligned with the recognised lines. Viewers can search it with Ctrl+F, selections copy actual words, and indexers can read it.

How accurate is the OCR?

On clean, straight, printed pages, typically above 98% of characters. Faded, skewed or decorative pages score lower, and handwriting is only partially recognised. Proofread anything critical — especially numbers.

Which language should I choose?

The language the document is actually written in — the wrong choice is the commonest cause of poor results. Mixed English + Hindi documents have a dedicated option.

How long does OCR take?

A few seconds per page on a typical device, plus a one-time engine and language download on first use. Progress is shown page by page, and recognised text accumulates as it goes.

Can OCR read handwriting?

Tesseract is built for print. Neat handwriting yields partial results; cursive and casual hands largely defeat it. Expect a helpful draft at best on handwritten pages.

Are my scanned documents uploaded for OCR?

No. The OCR engine downloads to your browser and runs on your device; your documents never leave your machine.
